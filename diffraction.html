Diffraction & Integration

What is Diffraction?

Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle. The amount of diffraction depends on the relationship between the wavelength of the wave and the size of the gap. When the gap is much larger than the wavelength, the wave spreads out very little. However, when the gap is similar in size to or smaller than the wavelength, the wave spreads out significantly. Longer wavelengths diffract more than shorter wavelengths, which is why sound waves can often be heard around corners while light waves generally travel in straight lines. Understanding diffraction helps explain how waves behave when they encounter obstacles and openings in their path.

About this Simulation

Diffraction is greatest when the slit width is approximately equal to the wavelength. As the slit becomes even narrower, the wavefront continues to spread out, but the transmitted wave becomes much weaker because less energy passes through the slit. This simulation shows the shape of the wavefront only, so the reduction in wave intensity is not represented (diffraction appears to increase).

Secondary Wavelets (Huygens' Principle)

Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of a new circular wavelet. The new wavefront is formed by the outer edge (envelope) of these secondary wavelets. When the wave passes through a slit, the wavelets spread out in all directions, producing the curved diffracted wavefront.

Understanding Two-Source Interference

What is interference?

Interference occurs when two or more waves overlap. While the waves overlap, their individual displacements combine according to the principle of superposition. This means that the resultant displacement at any point is the sum of the displacements produced by the individual waves at that point. After overlapping, the waves continue travelling; they do not permanently change or destroy one another.

Constructive and destructive interference

If two waves arrive in phase, their peaks meet peaks and their troughs meet troughs. Their displacements add to produce a wave with a larger amplitude. This is called constructive interference. Positions where constructive interference continually occurs form antinodal lines.

If two waves arrive out of phase, a peak from one wave meets a trough from the other. Their opposite displacements cancel, producing zero or minimum amplitude. This is called destructive interference. Positions where destructive interference continually occurs form nodal lines.

Why do lines of interference form?

The two sources in this diagram are coherent: they produce waves with the same frequency and a constant phase difference. A point in the diagram is usually a different distance from each source, so the two waves may travel different distances to reach it. This difference is called the path difference:

\[\text{path difference}=|r_2-r_1|\]

Here, \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are the distances travelled from the two sources. Because the sources are in phase, a whole-wavelength path difference makes the waves arrive in phase:

\[\text{constructive interference:}\quad |r_2-r_1|=n\lambda\]

In this equation, \(\lambda\) is the wavelength and \(n=0,1,2,3,\ldots\) is the whole-number order. For example, a path difference of \(2\lambda\) means one wave has travelled two complete wavelengths farther than the other. The waves are therefore still in phase when they meet.

An odd number of half-wavelengths makes the waves arrive in antiphase:

\[\text{destructive interference:}\quad |r_2-r_1|=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda\]

This produces path differences of \(0.5\lambda, 1.5\lambda, 2.5\lambda,\ldots\). Every point with the same required path difference belongs to the same nodal or antinodal line, which is why an interference pattern forms across the diagram rather than at only one point.

Young's interference equation

When the observation point is far from two sources separated by a distance \(d\), the path difference can be written as \(d\sin(\theta)\). Equating this geometric path difference to the constructive-interference condition gives Young's equation:

\[n\lambda=d\sin(\theta)\]

In this equation:

  • \(n\) is the order of the antinodal line: \(0,1,2,3,\ldots\);
  • \(\lambda\) is the wavelength;
  • \(d\) is the separation of the two sources; and
  • \(\theta\) is the angle between the central axis and the chosen antinodal line.

The central antinodal line has \(n=0\). Points on it are equally distant from both sources, so their path difference is zero. The first antinodal lines on either side have \(n=1\), the next pair have \(n=2\), and so on. Nodal directions can similarly be found using:

\[\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda=d\sin(\theta)\]

How this relates to a screen

Young's equation determines the angle of each antinodal line. If a screen were placed a perpendicular distance \(L\) from the sources, right-triangle geometry would determine where that line reaches the screen:

\[\tan(\theta)=\frac{x}{L}\]

Here, \(x\) is the distance from the central maximum to the selected maximum on the screen. This equation is not itself Young's interference equation; it is a geometric equation used alongside it. For small angles, \(\sin(\theta)\approx\tan(\theta)\), so the two equations can be combined:

\[x\approx\frac{n\lambda L}{d}\]

Therefore, for a fixed order \(n\), source separation \(d\), and screen distance \(L\), the displacement \(x\) is approximately directly proportional to wavelength. The spacing between neighbouring bright fringes is consequently:

\[\Delta x\approx\frac{\lambda L}{d}\]

What the interactive diagram demonstrates

The semicircular wavefronts represent waves spreading from the two sources. The peak and trough controls allow the matching and cancellation of the waves to be observed. The overlaid antinodal lines join positions where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, while the nodal lines join positions where it is an odd number of half- wavelengths.

Moving the wavelength slider changes \(\lambda\) while the source separation \(d\) remains constant. From \(n\lambda=d\sin(\theta)\), increasing the wavelength increases the angle \(\theta\) of each non-central order. The interference lines therefore spread farther apart and some may move beyond the visible diagram. If a screen were present, the maxima would appear farther from the centre and the fringe spacing would be greater. Decreasing the wavelength produces the opposite effect.

At larger angles, wavelength and screen displacement still increase together, but they are not exactly directly proportional because \(\sin(\theta)\) and \(\tan(\theta)\) change at different rates. The direct-proportion equation for \(x\) is an approximation that is most accurate when the screen is far away and the angles are small.

Simulation note: The diagram models each slit as a point source and does not display a physical screen or the intensity of the fringes. It demonstrates the positions and spacing of the nodal and antinodal lines rather than the brightness distribution of a real two-slit experiment.
