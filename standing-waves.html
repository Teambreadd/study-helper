Standing Waves

What are standing waves?

A standing wave is a stationary pattern formed when a wave and its reflection, travelling in opposite directions, interfere. Standing waves can form on stretched strings, pipes and in open or closed air columns. They contain fixed nodes, where displacement is zero, and antinodes, where displacement is greatest. Resonance occurs only at frequencies that fit an allowed number of half-wavelengths within the string or pipe, or an odd number of quarter-wavelengths in a pipe closed at one end. Each allowed pattern is called a harmonic.

Additional Information

What is the harmonic number?

The harmonic number identifies an allowed resonant frequency and standing-wave pattern. The first harmonic is the lowest possible resonant frequency and is also called the fundamental frequency. Higher harmonics contain more sections of the standing wave, so they have shorter wavelengths and higher frequencies.

For a fixed-fixed or open-open system, the length contains a whole number of half-wavelengths:

\(L = \frac{n\lambda}{2}\)

Therefore, the allowed frequencies are:

\(f_n = \frac{nv}{2L} = nf_1\)

Here, \(n\) is the harmonic number, \(v\) is the wave speed and \(L\) is the length of the string or air column. For example, the second harmonic has twice the fundamental frequency, while the third harmonic has three times the fundamental frequency.

Why does the fixed-open end boundary type only have odd harmonic numbers?

A fixed or closed end must be a displacement node, while a free or open end must be a displacement antinode. The shortest pattern that satisfies these two different boundary conditions contains one quarter of a wavelength:

\(L = \frac{\lambda_1}{4}\)

The next possible pattern must add another half-wavelength so that the fixed end remains a node and the open end remains an antinode. The allowed lengths are therefore:

\(L = \frac{\lambda}{4},\; \frac{3\lambda}{4},\; \frac{5\lambda}{4},\ldots\)

These correspond to the first, third, fifth and subsequent odd harmonics:

\(f_n = \frac{nv}{4L}, \qquad n = 1,3,5,\ldots\)

An even harmonic would place the wrong displacement condition at one of the ends, so it cannot form a resonant standing wave in a fixed-open system.

How can waves reflect at an open end?

When a sound wave reaches the open end of a pipe, it passes from the confined air inside the pipe into the much larger volume of air outside. This sudden change in the conditions through which the wave travels causes some of its energy to reflect back into the pipe. The remaining energy leaves the pipe as sound.

The pressure at the opening must remain close to atmospheric pressure. Therefore, the change in pressure is approximately zero at the open end, producing a pressure node. Pressure and air-particle displacement have opposite node and antinode positions, so the open end is also a displacement antinode.

Reflection at a real open end is not perfect, and the displacement antinode forms slightly beyond the physical end of the pipe. This small difference is called end correction and is ignored in this idealised simulation.

What happens at frequencies between the harmonics?

A string or air column can be driven at frequencies between its harmonic frequencies, and waves will still travel and reflect. However, the incident and reflected waves will not repeatedly reinforce one another in the same positions. A stable standing-wave pattern with fixed nodes and antinodes therefore does not form.

At a harmonic frequency, each reflection returns with the correct phase to reinforce the existing oscillation. Energy is transferred efficiently into the system, producing resonance and a large-amplitude standing wave.

Between harmonic frequencies, successive reflections are not consistently in phase. Constructive and destructive interference still occur, but their positions and strengths may change with time, and the response has a smaller amplitude.

Frequencies close to a harmonic can still produce a noticeable response, especially when there is damping. However, the greatest amplitude occurs at, or very close to, the resonant frequency.
